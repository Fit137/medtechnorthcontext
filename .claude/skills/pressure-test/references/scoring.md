# Scoring

Three numbers per belief. The point of scoring is not precision, it is forcing a ranking that survives being argued with.

---

## Load, 1 to 10

How much of the business stops working if this belief is false.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 9 to 10 | There is no business. The right response to being wrong is to stop and rebuild the plan |
| 7 to 8 | A core line closes, or the pricing model inverts. The company survives and is materially different |
| 5 to 6 | A quarter is lost, a channel closes, a segment turns out not to work. Painful, recoverable |
| 3 to 4 | A cost line moves, a plan gets slower |
| 1 to 2 | Should probably not be in the register |

**Calibration check:** if more than three beliefs score 9 or 10, the scale has drifted. Ask of each: would we still be doing this business at all? Only the ones where the answer is no belong at the top.

---

## Odds wrong, 1 to 10

An honest estimate against the evidence that exists, not against how much the plan needs it to be true.

Anchor against the evidence column rather than intuition:

| Evidence | Odds wrong is usually |
|---|---|
| Nothing. Reasoning only | 5 to 7 |
| Interviews where people said they would | 5 to 6. Stated intent predicts behaviour poorly |
| A handful of transactions, all warm or founder-led | 4 to 5 |
| Repeated transactions from cold sources | 2 to 3 |
| A result from an adjacent market or a credible analogue | 3 to 5, depending on how close the analogue is |
| Evidence that points the other way | 7 to 9. Say so plainly |

**Watch for the borrowed proof.** A frequent error is a belief supported by evidence that does not actually address it. Money changing hands proves willingness to pay for what was bought, not for what you plan to sell next. When the evidence and the belief are not the same claim, the odds wrong is closer to "nothing" than the founder thinks, and saying so is usually the most useful thing in the register.

## Hidden time, in months

How long the business could keep operating before reality would tell you anyway, if you did nothing deliberate.

This is the number nobody estimates and it moves the priority order more than the other two.

| Hidden time | Examples |
|---|---|
| Under 2 months | Nobody replies to the launch. Nobody buys. The build does not work |
| 3 to 9 months | The channel does not scale. The second segment does not convert. Unit economics under load |
| 12 to 24 months | Retention. Portability beyond the first market. Whether a gatekeeper appears at scale |
| Over 24 months | Competitive response. Concentration. Assumed constants nobody is watching |

**The rule this produces: a high-load belief with a hidden time of three weeks does not need a test, it needs three weeks.** Testing budget belongs to beliefs that hide. A belief that will announce itself shortly is already being tested by the calendar, for free.

---

## Pressure score and ranking

**Pressure score = Load x Odds wrong.** That is the damage, and it sorts the register.

**Priority adjusts pressure score by hidden time and test cost:**

- Long hidden time moves a belief up. Nothing else will tell you
- Short hidden time moves it down. Wait, and spend the effort elsewhere
- Cheap tests move up. A three-day test on a mid-ranked belief often beats a six-week test on the top one
- **One override beats all of the above:** a belief that gates a date or a commitment already made goes first, whatever it scores. Learning cheaply but too late is still too late

State the priority order explicitly in the register, and where it departs from raw pressure score, say why in a line. A reader who cannot see why number four is being tested before number one will assume the ranking is decorative.

## Worked examples

**A: "Hospitals will approve a purchase at this size without a formal procurement process."**
Load 8, the sales model and the cash forecast both assume a short cycle. Odds wrong 7, no purchase has completed and the belief rests on two informal conversations. Hidden time 9 months, because the veto appears at the point of purchase and every deal before then looks healthy. Pressure 56, and priority is raised because a gatekeeper pre-read costs two days.

**B: "People will pay for the premium tier."**
Load 9, the whole model rests on the mix. Odds wrong 5, some evidence exists from a small warm cohort. Hidden time 1 month, the paid launch is in three weeks. Pressure 45, and priority is lowered, because the launch answers it for free before any test could report.

**C: "Our largest channel keeps its current terms."**
Load 9, two thirds of customers arrive through it. Odds wrong 4, no signal either way. Hidden time 24 months, nobody is watching and there would be no warning. Pressure 36, and priority is raised sharply. This is the shape of belief that ends companies: moderate score, invisible, and unattended.

Example C is the reason hidden time is scored at all. On pressure score alone it sits mid-table and gets ignored for a year.

## Common scoring errors

**Everything lands on 6, 7 or 8.** The scoring did no work. Something in the list is a 3 and something is a 10. Find them and commit.

**Confusing hard with load-bearing.** A difficult engineering problem with a known solution is work, not a belief. Only score it if failure would change the plan rather than the schedule.

**Scoring the founder's confidence instead of the evidence.** Odds wrong tracks what exists in the world, not how sure anyone sounds. Confident founders with no evidence are the normal case, not an exception.

**Rounding hidden time toward the comfortable answer.** When unsure, take the longer estimate. The failure mode this whole exercise exists to prevent is finding out late.

**Scoring a belief nobody is acting on.** If the plan does not depend on it, it is context, not a register entry.

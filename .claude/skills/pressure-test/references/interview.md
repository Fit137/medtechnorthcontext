# The Interview

The register is only as good as this. Everything worth finding is in what the founder has not written down, and the difference between a useful pressure test and a generic one is almost entirely made here.

Contents: craft rules · eleven named moves · questions by phase · reading the answers · difficult interviews

---

## Why this carries the exercise

A founder's written material describes the business they intend to build. The interview is where you find the business they are actually running.

The gap between those two is where load-bearing beliefs live. Not because anyone is lying, but because the beliefs that matter most stopped feeling like claims a long time ago. "Dentists will pay for this" was a hypothesis in month one. By month nine it is furniture, it is in the pricing and the hiring plan, and nobody has questioned it since. Your job is to make the furniture visible again.

That does not happen by asking "what are your assumptions". Founders answer that question with the assumptions they have already examined, which are by definition the safe ones.

---

## Ten craft rules

**1. Never ask what the material already answers.** It signals you did not read, and the founder correctly downgrades every later question. If you need to confirm something you read, say where you read it: "your deck says forty pilots, is that signed or in conversation".

**2. Ask for the last instance, not the pattern.** "When did a customer last say that, and what were their exact words?" produces evidence. "Do customers say that?" produces a summary the founder has already told themselves many times, and rehearsed summaries have had all the useful detail worn off them.

**3. Ask about behaviour, not opinion.** "What did they do next?" beats "were they interested?". Interest is free. What someone did after the call is the data.

**4. Separate the number from its source.** Ask the number. Then ask where it came from. Do this as two questions, because merging them lets a modelled figure and a measured figure arrive wearing the same clothes.

**5. Make the disconfirming answer easy to give.** Say out loud that the weak version is useful: "if nobody has actually paid yet, say so, it changes what we test rather than whether this is worth doing". Founders hide thin evidence by reflex, and they stop hiding it once they can see the honest answer is not being scored.

**6. Batch factual questions. Isolate uncomfortable ones.** Three to five at a time is efficient for facts. Put a hard question in a batch and it is the one that gets skipped, usually without either of you noticing.

**7. Follow the flinch.** When an answer gets shorter, vaguer, hedged, or jumps to a different subject, stay there for one more question than feels comfortable. That is where the belief is.

**8. Never lead.** "How painful is that for them?" presupposes pain and hands the founder the answer you wanted. "What do they do about it today?" does not.

**9. Ask what would change their mind before you argue with them.** It gets a real answer, and if you later present evidence, they have already committed to what would count.

**10. Ask the question you think you know the answer to.** You are often wrong, and the times you are wrong are the most valuable minutes of the interview.

## Eleven named moves

Use these deliberately. Each one surfaces a category of belief that direct questioning tends to miss.

**The pre-mortem.** "It is eighteen months from now and this failed. Not dramatically, it just did not work. What does the post-mortem say?" The single highest-yield question available. It gives permission to voice the doubt the founder already holds, and the first answer is almost always a real load-bearing belief.

**The bet.** "What odds would you take on that being true? Would you put ten thousand dollars of your own money on it at even money?" Converts confident language into a number. Watch for the gap between what someone says and what they would stake.

**The smartest critic.** "Who is the most credible person who thinks this will not work, and what is their actual argument?" Insist on the real version, not a strawman. If they cannot name one, that is itself a finding: it usually means the plan has only been shown to people who wanted it to work.

**The stopped clock.** "What have you stopped checking because the answer used to be fine?" Beliefs decay quietly. This finds the metric that was true in March and has not been looked at since.

**The invisible veto.** "Who has to say yes who is not in your pitch?" Procurement, IT, compliance, a regulator, a spouse, a platform, an insurer, a works council, a franchise head office. Gatekeeper beliefs are the most commonly missed category and this question is how you find them.

**The substitution.** "What do they do today instead, and what does that cost them?" Nobody has no solution. The real competitor is usually a spreadsheet, an assistant, or doing nothing, and the price of that is the price you are actually competing with.

**The last no.** "What did the last person who said no actually say, in their own words?" Founders remember their yeses in detail and summarise their nos into one flat sentence. The specific wording of a refusal is worth more than three yeses.

**Halve it.** "Which number in your model, if it halved, breaks the business?" Then: "how confident are you in that number, and where did it come from?" This finds the load-bearing assumption inside the financial model in about ninety seconds.

**The constant.** "What are you assuming stays the same for the next two years?" Prices, regulation, a platform's terms, a channel's cost, a partner's willingness, a founder's availability. Assumed constants are beliefs that never got written down because they were never decided.

**Hidden time.** "If this belief turned out to be false, how long could you keep operating before you noticed?" Founders almost never estimate this and it changes the priority order more than any other input. Ask it for every belief that scores high on load.

**The bus test.** "Which of this only works because you personally are doing it?" Concentration is a load-bearing belief that dresses up as a work ethic.

## Phase 1. Orientation

Six to eight questions. Cover ground fast, and note where you will come back.

1. In two or three sentences, what does the business do and who pays for it?
2. What is the transaction? Who hands over money, how much, how often, and what do they get?
3. What has actually happened so far? Not the plan. Money received, things signed, people using it, with dates.
4. What is the single sentence version of why this works? "We believe X, therefore Y."
5. Who is the customer you know best, and how did they find you?
6. What decision are you trying to make right now? What would you do differently depending on the answer?
7. What is already settled and not up for debate? *(This stops the exercise relitigating decisions the founder has correctly closed.)*
8. What is the runway, in months, and what has to be true before it runs out?

Question 3 is where most of the work happens. Founders answer it with intentions. Press until every item has a date and a number, and note anything that arrives without either.

## Phase 2. Surfacing

Walk the categories in `hypothesis-map.md`. That file carries the surfacing question for each one. Do not read the list aloud as a checklist, the interview should feel like a conversation, but do make sure every category gets touched, because the missed ones are usually gatekeeper, supply, portability and concentration.

Open with the pre-mortem. It shortcuts twenty minutes.

Then, whatever the domain, these five earn their place in nearly every interview:

- What do they do today instead, and what does it cost them?
- Who has to say yes who is not in your pitch?
- What are you assuming stays the same?
- Which number in your model, if halved, breaks it?
- What would your best customer say if I asked them why they really bought?

That last one is a good test of whether the founder has ever asked. Many have not, and "I assume because..." is a finding.

---

## Phase 3. Pressure

For each belief that looks load-bearing, four questions. This is where a candidate becomes a register entry.

1. **What makes you believe this?** Then keep going until you reach an observation rather than an inference. "Customers told us" is an inference. "Three of the eleven people we spoke to in March asked when they could buy" is an observation.
2. **What would you expect to see if it were false?** If they cannot answer, the belief is not yet testable and the first job is to make it testable. This question often does more than the test itself.
3. **How long could this be false before you noticed?** The hidden time score.
4. **What is the cheapest thing that would tell you?** Ask before you propose anything. Founders frequently know, and their version is usually better calibrated to their own constraints than yours.

---

## Phase 4. Confirm

Read each belief back in your own words, in one plain sentence, and let the founder correct the wording before anything is scored.

Two reasons this is not a formality. Wording drift is real, and a belief phrased slightly wrong gets tested slightly wrong. And a founder reading their own belief in someone else's words often revises it substantially, which is information you would not otherwise get.

Then ask the closing question: **"What did I not ask about that I should have?"** It routinely produces one more belief, and it is often the biggest.

## Reading the answers

| What you hear | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "Everyone we talk to loves it" | No structured conversation has happened, or only friendly ones have | Ask for the last three, by name and date, and what each did next |
| "We just need to get in front of more people" | An access belief is being treated as an effort problem | Ask what the last hundred contacts cost and produced |
| "It is obvious once you see it" | The founder has stopped being able to model a sceptical buyer | Ask about the smartest critic |
| "That is not really a risk for us" | It may be the biggest one | Ask what would have to be true for it to become one |
| A number with no source | Modelled, not measured | Ask where it came from, then score the belief underneath it |
| A very fast, very fluent answer | Rehearsed for investors, which means unexamined for a while | Ask for the last instance, which is never in the rehearsed version |
| "We will figure that out later" | A real belief with a long hidden time | Ask how much gets built before later arrives |
| Silence, or a change of subject | You found something | Ask one more question |

**A specific, ugly answer is worth ten polished ones.** When you get one, say so. It reinforces exactly the behaviour that makes the rest of the interview useful.

## Difficult interviews

**The founder in a hurry.** Ask the pre-mortem, the invisible veto, and "what has actually happened, with dates". Those three alone support a rough register. Stamp everything else unconfirmed and list the three questions that would most change the document.

**The rehearsed founder** answers in pitch paragraphs. Break the rhythm with specifics: a date, a name, an exact quote, a number and its source. Pitch language cannot survive a request for the last instance.

**The defensive founder** hears the exercise as a judgment. Reframe once, plainly: the aim is to find what to test first, not to score the business, and the strongest companies have the same number of unproven beliefs as the weak ones, they just know which ones they are. Then move to concrete questions, because abstraction is what feels like judgment.

**The founder with no data.** Common and fine, especially pre-launch. The register becomes almost entirely beliefs with no evidence, which is the honest picture, and the test plan becomes the whole product of the exercise. Do not manufacture evidence to make the register look balanced.

**The founder who wants you to agree.** The exercise fails silently here. If everything you surface gets absorbed and reframed as already-handled, say plainly that you have not yet found a belief they disagree with you about, and ask which one on the list they think you have overrated. That question restarts a real conversation.

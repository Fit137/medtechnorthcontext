# The Test Library

Seventeen ways to find out whether a belief holds, none of which require the product to exist and most of which run in under three weeks.

Contents: the two moves that come first · selection table · the seventeen archetypes · designing the kill number

---

## Before you design anything, two moves

**1. Check whether the experiment has already been run.** An adjacent industry, another country, a public post-mortem, a company that tried it in 2019 and wrote about why it failed, a trade body's survey, an academic result, a competitor's abandoned product line. The cheapest test in existence is reading someone else's result, and it takes an afternoon. Run this pass on every belief before designing anything original.

**2. Prefer days over quarters.** A rough answer in two weeks usually beats a clean answer in four months, because the plan is being executed against in the meantime. A weaker instrument that reports before the next decision is worth more than a stronger one that reports after it.

## The archetypes

### 1. The direct ask

**Tests:** demand, access, willingness, permission.
**Run it:** request the actual thing in doubt, from people you have no relationship with, in the words you would really use. Not a meeting about the thing. The thing.
**Pass looks like:** people agreeing to the specific request, at a rate you wrote down beforehand.
**How it lies:** a request for a meeting substitutes for the real ask, and you measure curiosity. Warm contacts substitute for cold ones, and you measure your own reputation.
**Cost:** days, near zero money.

*The rule underneath every other test on this page: the thing you request has to be the thing in doubt.*

### 2. The pre-sale

**Tests:** willingness to pay.
**Run it:** ask for money, a signature, a deposit or a dated commitment before the thing exists. Be explicit that it does not exist yet and give a refund path.
**Pass looks like:** money actually arriving, or a signature from someone with authority.
**How it lies:** friendly buyers purchase the founder rather than the product. Discount the result heavily for anyone in your network, and run at least half the sample cold.
**Cost:** days to weeks, near zero money.

### 3. Concierge

**Tests:** value delivery, feasibility, unit economics.
**Run it:** deliver the outcome completely by hand for three to five customers, with no product at all. Charge for it.
**Pass looks like:** they get the outcome, they say so without being prompted, and they buy again.
**How it lies:** the founder delivers with founder energy and unpaid hours, so unit economics look better than they are. Log your own time at a real rate.
**Cost:** weeks, real time.

### 4. Wizard of Oz

**Tests:** demand and value, without building.
**Run it:** the front looks finished, the back is people. Users do not need to know how it works, only that it does.
**Pass looks like:** repeat usage without prompting.
**How it lies:** manual quality exceeds what the automated version will achieve, so you validate a product you cannot ship. Note which parts of the delivery are genuinely hard to automate.
**Cost:** weeks.

### 5. Fake door

**Tests:** demand and message.
**Run it:** advertise the thing that does not exist, measure who tries to get it, then tell everyone honestly and offer to keep them posted.
**Pass looks like:** a click-to-attempt rate you set in advance, and people replying to ask when.
**How it lies:** it measures curiosity, not commitment, and it is the easiest test to over-read. Treat it as a filter for what to test properly, never as proof.
**Cost:** days, small money.
**Note:** be straight with people who arrive. A fake door that leaves someone feeling tricked costs more than the test is worth.

### 6. Held-price A/B

**Tests:** which promise actually pulls, and therefore what you are really selling.
**Run it:** one list, split at random before sending, same price and same offer in both arms, only the promise changes. Assignment fixed in advance.
**Pass looks like:** a clear difference in qualified responses between arms.
**How it lies:** varying price and promise together, which makes the result unreadable. Hold the money still.
**Cost:** days.

### 7. Price ladder

**Tests:** willingness to pay, ceiling, and where the objection lands.
**Run it:** the same offer at three ascending prices to comparable segments. Watch where the objection appears rather than where the yes does.
**Pass looks like:** finding the price at which the conversation changes character.
**How it lies:** segments are not comparable, so you measure segment and call it price.
**Cost:** weeks.
**Read it this way:** if nobody objects to the price, it is too low. That is a finding, not a success.

### 8. The falsifying question

**Tests:** any belief about the buyer's world.
**Run it:** ask the question whose honest answer would prove you wrong, directly, of strangers, and count unprompted concessions. Signal that the disconfirming answer is welcome.
**Pass looks like:** a majority conceding the thing your business depends on.
**How it lies:** the question is phrased so the polite answer is the one you wanted. Have someone else read it before sending.
**Cost:** days.

### 9. Switch interview

**Tests:** trigger, demand, competitive position.
**Run it:** find people who recently changed from the incumbent to anything else. Ask what happened in the week before they switched, not why they switched.
**Pass looks like:** a repeated, specific trigger event across several people.
**How it lies:** people construct tidy reasons after the fact. Anchor on the timeline and on what happened, not on motives.
**Cost:** weeks.

### 10. Reverse pilot

**Tests:** value, retention, what is actually load-bearing in the offer.
**Run it:** remove a feature, a service element or a channel for a defined group, and see who complains.
**Pass looks like:** silence, which means it was not load-bearing, or a fast complaint, which means it was.
**How it lies:** silence can mean nobody noticed because nobody is using any of it. Check engagement before concluding.
**Cost:** days, some goodwill.

### 11. Supply dry run

**Tests:** supply, portability.
**Run it:** try to source three times the current volume of the scarce input, before you need it, and stop before committing.
**Pass looks like:** the input arriving at a cost and lead time the plan can carry.
**How it lies:** you use existing relationships, which are exactly the thing that will not scale. Source cold for at least half.
**Cost:** days to weeks.

### 12. Gatekeeper pre-read

**Tests:** gatekeepers, regulation, trust.
**Run it:** put the plan in front of the person who could veto it, before building. A security reviewer, a procurement lead, a compliance officer, a regulator's guidance line, a professional body, a platform's policy team.
**Pass looks like:** a clear path, or a clear list of what is required.
**How it lies:** an informal opinion from someone without authority reads as approval. Ask specifically who signs and what form the decision takes.
**Cost:** days to weeks, near zero money.
**Underused.** This is usually the cheapest test on the page against the belief with the longest hidden time.

### 13. Cohort probe

**Tests:** retention.
**Run it:** define a cohort, instrument it, and wait the actual window. When the window is longer than the runway, find someone who already ran it: a competitor's disclosed numbers, an adjacent category's benchmarks, an operator willing to tell you.
**Pass looks like:** a curve that flattens where the model needs it to.
**How it lies:** the earliest cohort is your most enthusiastic customers and retains far better than the next one will.
**Cost:** the length of the window, which is why analogue research usually comes first.

### 14. Manual first delivery

**Tests:** unit economics, feasibility, value.
**Run it:** deliver the whole thing once, end to end, and record every hour and every dollar, including your own time at a rate you would have to pay someone else.
**Pass looks like:** a real cost number you can put in the model.
**How it lies:** the first delivery is unusually careful and unusually slow. Do it twice if the number is load-bearing.
**Cost:** one delivery.

### 15. Second market probe

**Tests:** portability, access, concentration.
**Run it:** run the same offer in a different city, segment or channel where you have no warm list and no reputation.
**Pass looks like:** a response rate within a defined distance of home.
**How it lies:** the founder works the second market harder than the first because it is the test, which flatters the result.
**Cost:** weeks.

### 16. The bus test

**Tests:** concentration.
**Run it:** deliberately remove the founder from one deal or one delivery, end to end. Someone else sends the messages, takes the call, does the work.
**Pass looks like:** it closes or ships anyway.
**How it lies:** the founder stays involved invisibly. Agree the boundary in advance and hold it even when the deal wobbles.
**Cost:** one deal, and some nerve.

### 17. Analogue research

**Tests:** anything, cheaply, first.
**Run it:** find where this experiment already ran. Another country, an adjacent category, a company that tried it and stopped, a trade body's data, a regulator's consultation responses, a public post-mortem.
**Pass looks like:** a result specific enough to move a score, with a named source.
**How it lies:** the analogue is not as close as it looks. Write down explicitly what is different, and if the differences are load-bearing, the analogue is context rather than evidence.
**Cost:** an afternoon.

## Designing the kill number

The number that decides the verdict, written before the test runs. A threshold chosen after the results arrive is a rationalisation with a percentage sign on it, and everyone involved will know.

**Set three numbers, not one:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Kill** | At or below this, the belief is broken and the plan changes |
| **Pass** | At or above this, the belief holds for now |
| **The gap between them** | Inconclusive, which means the sample was too small or the ask was wrong. Say which |

Naming the inconclusive band in advance is what stops a middling result being read as a pass.

**Sizing the sample when there is no baseline.** Most early companies have no historical rate to compare against, which makes statistical framing false precision. Use a decision rule instead: what is the smallest result that would make you change the plan? That number is the kill number, and the sample is however many attempts it takes for that number to be meaningful to you. Twenty to sixty attempts is the usual range for an ask-based test.

**Make a no informative.** "Not right now" is noise. "We already do this in-house and it works" is a result. Design the question so refusals carry a reason, and record the reason every time. A no with a reason moves a score. A no without one means the question needs rewriting.

**Fix the date the verdict gets read**, and read it on that date even when the result is disappointing and even when the test could run a little longer. Extending a test that is going badly is the most common way a kill number gets quietly abandoned.

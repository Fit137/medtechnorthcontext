# The Category Map

Fourteen categories of load-bearing belief. Every business rests on all fourteen, whether or not anyone has written them down.

Use this in Phase 2 as a coverage check rather than a script. Touch every category, spend time where the answers get vague, and note that the categories founders most often skip are **gatekeeper, supply, portability and concentration**, while the one they most over-test is **demand**, because demand is the fun one and the one investors ask about.

---

## How to read each entry

**The belief** is the generic form. Rewrite it in the founder's own specifics before it goes in the register.
**Surfacing question** is what you actually ask.
**The classic wrong version** is how this belief usually fails, across industries.
**Test family** points at `test-library.md`.

---

### 1. Demand

**The belief:** Someone wants this enough to change what they currently do.

**Surfacing question:** What do they do about this problem today, and what does that cost them in money, time or hassle?

**The classic wrong version:** The problem is real and widely felt, and it is also comfortably tolerated. Nobody is looking for a solution because the current workaround is annoying rather than expensive. A tolerated problem generates warm interviews and no purchases.

**Test family:** Direct ask · fake door · switch interview

---

### 2. Willingness to pay

**The belief:** They will pay, at this price, from this budget, to us.

**Surfacing question:** Which budget does this come out of, who owns it, and what is currently being bought with it?

**The classic wrong version:** Demand is real and there is no budget line for it, so every purchase requires a new one to be created, which is a different and much slower sale. Distinct from demand, and confusing the two is the most common single error in this exercise.

**Test family:** Pre-sale · price ladder · held-price A/B

---

### 3. Value delivery

**The belief:** What we deliver actually produces the outcome they bought.

**Surfacing question:** For the customer who has had this longest, what changed for them, and how do you know?

**The classic wrong version:** The product does what it says and the outcome does not follow, because something else in the customer's world was the real constraint. Shows up as good usage, good reviews and no renewal.

**Test family:** Concierge · manual first delivery · reverse pilot

---

### 4. Access

**The belief:** We can reach the buyer, repeatably, at a cost that works.

**Surfacing question:** How did the last ten customers actually arrive, one by one, and what did each one cost to get?

**The classic wrong version:** The first customers came through the founder's network, which is not a channel, and the plan assumes a repeatable version of it exists. This is the belief most often mistaken for an effort problem: "we just need more conversations".

**Test family:** Direct ask · channel probe · second market probe

---

### 5. Supply and inputs

**The belief:** We can source the scarce input at volume.

**Surfacing question:** What is the hardest thing to get more of, and what happens when you need three times as much of it?

**The classic wrong version:** The scarce input was abundant at ten units and is rationed at a hundred. The input might be people, inventory, data, clinicians, drivers, moderators, licences, shelf space or the founder's own relationships. Businesses that assemble people are especially exposed here and rarely test it.

**Test family:** Supply dry run · analogue research

---

### 6. Feasibility

**The belief:** We can actually build or do this, at quality, at the scale the plan assumes.

**Surfacing question:** What is the part of this you have not built or done yet, and who has done something equivalent?

**The classic wrong version:** The hard part is known and the plan quietly assumes it takes one quarter because a competitor appears to have done it in one quarter.

**Test family:** Manual first delivery · concierge · analogue research

---

### 7. Unit economics

**The belief:** The money works per unit, not only in aggregate at scale.

**Surfacing question:** Take the last unit you delivered. What did it actually cost, counting your own hours at a real rate?

**The classic wrong version:** Costs that are currently free because the founder absorbs them. The unit is profitable while unpaid labour is invisible, and it goes negative the moment the work is done by someone who has to be paid.

**Test family:** Manual first delivery · halve-it stress on the model

---

### 8. Retention and repeat

**The belief:** They come back, or buy again, or stay long enough for the lifetime value to be real.

**Surfacing question:** What is the longest anyone has been a customer, and what is your evidence about what happens at month twelve?

**The classic wrong version:** Every lifetime value figure is a projection, and the retention curve has never been observed past the age of the oldest customer. Universal in companies under two years old, and it is load-bearing whenever acquisition spend is justified by lifetime value.

**Test family:** Cohort probe · analogue research · reverse pilot

---

### 9. Gatekeepers

**The belief:** Nobody outside the buying conversation can block this.

**Surfacing question:** Who has to say yes who is not in your pitch?

**The classic wrong version:** The buyer is enthusiastic and cannot proceed. Security review, procurement, legal, a regulator, an insurer, a platform's policy, a union agreement, a franchisor, a professional body, a spouse. This is the most commonly missed category in this entire map, and the one with the longest hidden time, because the veto usually appears only at the point of purchase.

**Test family:** Gatekeeper pre-read · analogue research

---

### 10. Trust and permission

**The belief:** They will let us be the ones to do this.

**Surfacing question:** Why you? What makes someone comfortable buying this from a company of your size and age?

**The classic wrong version:** The offer is good and the category requires a credential, a track record, an insurance certificate or an institutional name that the company does not have. Especially load-bearing in health, finance, education, children's services, security and anything touching a professional body.

**Test family:** Direct ask · gatekeeper pre-read · held-price A/B

---

### 11. Timing and trigger

**The belief:** There is a reason to act now rather than next year.

**Surfacing question:** What changed recently that makes this urgent, and what happens to the buyer if they do nothing for twelve months?

**The classic wrong version:** The value is real and evergreen, so the purchase can always be deferred one more quarter. Deals do not die, they slip, indefinitely. If there is no dated external trigger, one has to be manufactured honestly, usually through a fixed date or a closing cohort.

**Test family:** Switch interview · direct ask with a dated close

---

### 12. Portability and scale

**The belief:** This works beyond the first customer, city, segment or channel.

**Surfacing question:** What is specifically true about the customers you have that might not be true of the next hundred?

**The classic wrong version:** The first market worked because of something local and unnamed: the founder's relationships, one city's regulation, one segment's unusual budget cycle. The plan assumes replication and has never tested it anywhere cold.

**Test family:** Second market probe · direct ask in a cold segment

---

### 13. Competitive response

**The belief:** When a larger player notices, we still have a business.

**Surfacing question:** If the obvious incumbent copied you exactly next quarter, what still stops them?

**The classic wrong version:** The moat is being first, or being faster, and neither survives attention. Note this is a belief and not a risk when the plan already assumes a period of being ignored.

**Test family:** Analogue research · reverse pilot

---

### 14. Concentration

**The belief:** The business is not resting on a single point that can be removed.

**Surfacing question:** Which of this only works because you personally are doing it? And what share of revenue comes from your largest customer, channel or supplier?

**The classic wrong version:** One founder holds the selling, the delivery and the relationships. One channel supplies most of the customers. One customer is a third of revenue. It looks like efficiency and it behaves like a single point of failure. Rarely written down because it feels like a fact about the present rather than a bet on the future.

**Test family:** Bus test · second market probe

---

## Cross-cutting: the assumed constants

Not a category so much as a sweep to run at the end. Ask what the plan assumes stays the same for two years, then check each answer against the categories above.

Common ones: a channel's cost per acquisition, a platform's terms and take rate, a regulation, a price, an interest or exchange rate, a partner's willingness, a supplier's capacity, a key person's availability, a competitor's inattention, the cost of capital.

Assumed constants earn their own register entries when the plan cannot survive them moving. They score high on hidden time almost by definition, because nobody is watching them.

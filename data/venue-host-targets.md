**The format this list serves.** One room, 50 to 100 people, a stage, multiple panels and recorded interviews. Named speakers with their faces on the invitation are the acquisition engine, because an anonymous guest list cannot be advertised and a named speaker can. This is a **separate path from the Table series**, which continues unchanged at its own size.

## The screen, six checks

| # | Requirement | Why it decides |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **One room seating 50 to 100, theatre or cabaret** | The whole format. This replaces the old six-room requirement |
| 2 | **A stage or presentation front, with house AV**: screen, projector or LED, at least four mics | Panels need mics. Hiring AV puts the cash risk back |
| 3 | **A second space for filmed interviews** | Interviews run in parallel with the programme. A quiet room, not a studio |
| 4 | **Reception or lobby space** for arrival and the hour afterwards | Where the actual value happens, and where the footage of a full room comes from |
| 5 | **No procurement path to a yes** | Nothing is bought, so the test is whether one named person owns the calendar |
| 6 | **A weekday inside a two-week window in October or November** | Ask for a window. Facilities say yes to windows and no to dates |

**Disqualifier that matters more than it looks:** any venue whose business model is renting the room. There we are a customer, the conversation is a price negotiation, and the free yes does not exist. MaRS is the clearest example and it is why it scores where it does despite having the best facilities on the list.

## Scoring, and what each axis means

Four axes, 100 points, one penalty. Every score is a judgement recorded so it can be argued with, not a measurement. Change a number and say why.

| Axis | Out of | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| **V. Venue fit** | 30 | Room for 50 to 100 with a stage (15), house AV (7), interview room (5), reception space (3) |
| **F. Friction to yes** | 25 | One person owns the calendar with no procurement (10), external events are routine (6), no unusual legal or insurance hurdle (5), a timing incentive live in Oct and Nov (4) |
| **P. Patriotic and mandate pull** | 20 | Building the Canadian health ecosystem is their funding basis or literal mandate (10), Canadian-owned or a public body (5), a reporting obligation this event satisfies (5) |
| **ROI. Commercial pull** | 25 | The room contains their buyers or their hires (10), they hold budget to fund as well as host (8), the media is worth something to them (7) |
| **Riv. Rivalry penalty** | 0, -10, -20 | Whether they could read this as competing with something they sell. See the rivalry section |

**Read the total as a ranking, not a verdict.** The gap between 79 and 73 is noise. The gap between 73 and 44 is not.

**Venue column:** `Y` a facility is known or near-certain for the category. `?` plausible and unverified. `N` no venue, so they are a speaker or funder candidate rather than a host.

## What the ranking actually says

**Banks take four of the top thirteen**, which was not the expected result. They own large downtown auditoriums, they run external events weekly so the machinery exists, the big five are Canadian-owned so the patriotic argument lands properly rather than awkwardly, and Scotiabank Healthcare+ runs dedicated programmes for dentists and pharmacists ✅, which means the room is literally their customer acquisition target. Scotiabank is the highest-scoring name on the page and it earned it on four independent axes.

**Colleges and polytechnics beat universities on friction**, consistently, by three to five points. They are more commercially minded, industry engagement is a stated purpose rather than a side activity, and the yes usually needs one office rather than two. George Brown and Humber sit above every university except McMaster.

**McMaster is the highest-scoring institution and it also has the only warm door on the list.** A professor there posted one of the four public LinkedIn testimonials. That is worth more than the three points separating it from the next university.

**Hospitals cluster at 59 to 62 and the scoring is misleading.** Friction drags them down and it is real: research ethics, communications review, slow calendars. But they carry the highest legitimacy of any category, which is the thing that recruits speakers. **Treat hospitals as the primary speaker source and a secondary venue source**, and the score stops being the point.

**Associations are at the bottom and the score is doing its job.** Medtech Canada at 21 and BIOTECanada at 20 are not weak organisations. They are organisations whose product is the thing being built, with no venue to offer. Approach them for speakers, never for a room.

**The medtech corridor holds up.** Mississauga alone puts PointClickCare, Baylis, GE HealthCare and Myant in the top eleven, all within a short drive, on top of the corridor facts already recorded: over 500 life sciences businesses, more than 27,000 skilled workers, and over $1.2 billion in life sciences research and development foreign direct investment across five years ✅.

## The rivalry question, which is the right question

You named MaRS and Medtech Canada. The instinct is correct and the test is not "are we competitors", it is **"does this organisation sell something that a 50 to 100 person health innovation event with panels would compete with"**. Run that test and the categories separate cleanly.

### Where the rivalry is real

| Organisation type | What they sell | Why you look like a threat |
|---|---|---|
| **MaRS** | Tenancy, programmes, **event sponsorship**, and their own health innovation events inside Toronto Health Innovation Week ✅ | Your event is a smaller version of theirs, and **their sponsors are your funders**. You are competing for the same corporate budget from the same companies. They also rent space commercially, so a free yes was never available |
| **Medtech Canada** | Membership dues from device manufacturers, plus their own conferences and sponsorship | Their members are your corporate targets. Member value is their product, and an outside convener assembling their members reads as disintermediation |
| **Life Sciences Ontario, OBIO, BIOTECanada, LifeSciences BC** | Same shape at a smaller scale | Same reasoning |
| **Regional innovation centres** | Provincially funded, report on companies served, several take sponsorship | Milder. Territorial rather than commercial, and it depends entirely on framing |

### Where there is no rivalry at all

Banks, insurers, platforms, medtech and pharma headquarters, professional services firms, universities, colleges, hospitals, and economic development bodies. **None of them sells convening.** They convene as marketing, as mandate, or not at all. A firm like Deloitte runs a Greenhouse as client marketing, not as a revenue line, so an outside convener is a channel rather than a competitor.

This is the strongest argument for your own preference, and it holds.

### How to address it, in five moves

1. **Never ask a rival for a venue. Ask them for a speaker.** It converts a territorial request into a flattering one, and a panellist from an association becomes invested in the event succeeding rather than threatened by it.
2. **Do not use their vocabulary.** Network, ecosystem, community and platform are the words their own funding applications are written in. Say "one evening, one room, one question, this date". A dated single event is not a competing organisation and it should not be described as one.
3. **Lead with what they cannot assemble.** For Medtech Canada that is practising clinicians, which is the gap in every manufacturer-side body. For MaRS it is depth in one specialty rather than breadth across health innovation.
4. **Approach them last, not first.** After an institutional venue is signed and two or three speakers are named. Then you are offering participation in something real rather than asking for help launching something that competes with them.
5. **Accept that some of them are simply not on the list.** An organisation that would be a rival and cannot be a partner is not a loss. There are 88 names here.

## Are you wrong to prefer commercial partners with budget

**Half right, and the half that is wrong is the expensive half.**

**Where you are right.** Commercial hosts carry zero rivalry risk, they hold budget so the same relationship can fund as well as host, their facilities are real, one person can approve, and a 31 December fiscal year makes October and November the best-timed ask in the calendar. Four of the top six names on this list are commercial for exactly these reasons.

**Where the preference costs you.** Your own stated engine is named speakers, because a dinner with an anonymous list cannot be advertised and a panel with faces can. Now ask what actually recruits a speaker.

A hospital innovation lead, a dean, or a professor accepts an invitation on institutional legitimacy. **A first-time 50 to 100 person event with panels, hosted inside a bank or a software company, reads as a vendor event**, and that is the single hardest thing to recruit a clinical speaker into. Hosted at a university or a hospital, the same event reads as an institutional convening and the same person says yes. The venue is not just a room here, it is the first line of the speaker pitch.

Two further costs, both structural:

**Category exclusivity gets tight.** At a 25-person dinner, one funder holding a category is clean. At 100 people across five panels you need many companies represented, and a commercial host constrains who can credibly be on stage. If Microsoft hosts, an AWS panellist is awkward. A university host has no such problem.

**Legitimacy transfers one way.** An institutional venue makes commercial funders easier to sell. A commercial venue does not make institutional speakers easier to recruit. Sequencing matters and it only works in one direction.

**The resolution, which is already the structure in `ADR-025`.** Split the asks and let each side do what it is good at.

| Ask | Best source | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **The venue** | University, college, or hospital | Legitimacy, real theatres, no rivalry, mandate to host |
| **The money** | Bank, platform, medtech, insurer | Budget, fiscal timing, buyers in the room |
| **The speakers** | Hospitals, universities, clinical leaders | The names that make the event advertisable |

Run the commercial list for funding in parallel and lose nothing. The one thing not to do is take the first commercial venue offered and discover in October that the speakers you wanted will not stand on that stage.

**One exception worth naming.** Scotiabank scores highest for a reason that survives this argument: the attendees are its acquisition target, so it can fund generously, and it has no stake whatsoever in any clinical conclusion. If a commercial host is going to work, that shape is the one. It still does not solve the speaker problem.

## Verification protocol, five minutes per candidate

The seat count is the field that decides everything and it is the one field nobody has. Collect it before scoring is worth trusting.

**Look for, in this order:** a room-booking or conference page on their own site, a published floor plan or capacity chart, an events or facility-rental page, photographs of a past event at their own address, a named events or facilities coordinator on LinkedIn.

**What to record:**

| Field | Values |
|---|---|
| `seats_theatre` | Integer. The number that decides fit |
| `seats_cabaret` | Integer. Usually 50 to 60 percent of theatre |
| `stage` | `fixed` · `riser` · `floor` · `none` |
| `av_in_house` | `full` · `partial` · `none` · `unknown`. Partial means we hire mics |
| `mics` | Count of handheld and lav available |
| `interview_room` | `yes` · `no` · `unknown` |
| `reception_space` | `yes` · `no` · `unknown` |
| `catering` | `in-house required` · `preferred list` · `open` |
| `cost_signal` | `free` · `at cost` · `rental card` · `unknown`. A rental card moves them toward disqualified |
| `named_contact` | Name and title |
| `owns_calendar` | Whether that person can say yes alone |
| `rivalry` | `none` · `low` · `medium` · `high` |
| `category_stake` | `orthogonal` · `adjacent` · `conflicted`. Separate from rivalry: this is whether their product biases the panel content |
| `evidence` | URL per non-obvious claim |

**Two fields people conflate, kept apart deliberately.** Rivalry is whether they compete with you as a convener. Category stake is whether their product bends what gets said on stage. Microsoft has zero rivalry and low stake. TELUS Health has medium rivalry and high stake. They are different problems with different fixes.

**Work order.** Verify the top twenty first. Twenty verified seat counts are worth more than eighty-eight scored guesses, and roughly a third of the `?` rows will fail check 1 and drop out, which is the point of checking.

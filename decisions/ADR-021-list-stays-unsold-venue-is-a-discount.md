# ADR-021: The list stays unsold. The venue is a discount, not a premium

**Status:** Accepted

## Part 1. The attendee list

**Decision: never a paid deliverable, before or after the room, to anyone.**

### The decisive reason is legal, not philosophical
Under **PIPEDA**, personal information may only be disclosed for purposes the individual consented to. A clinician registering for a dinner consents to attending a dinner. Handing their name, employer and email to a commercial third party is disclosure to a third party for marketing, which needs **separate, informed, meaningful consent** obtained up front. Under **CASL**, the recipient of that list then needs express or implied consent to email them. Implied consent from a genuine business-card exchange is narrow and does not extend to a supplied list.

`docs/08-legal-and-compliance.md` already forbids anything about member data that conflicts with PIPEDA consent. This is that.

### The commercial reason, which matters as much
There are two businesses here and only one is defensible.

| | Lead-list business | Composition business |
|---|---|---|
| What is sold | Contacts | That these 24 people were in one room and no competitor was |
| Priced against | Conference lists, data vendors, Sales Navigator | Nothing. There is no comparable |
| Ceiling | Low, and falls as the market matures | Set by scarcity of the room |
| Replicable | Yes, by anyone | No |

**The moment the list is the deliverable, the price is set by the count of names.** Selling the list does not strengthen the funder price, it caps it.

### The trust mechanism, stated plainly
The free side attends **because it is not a lead-generation event**. If a clinician attends once and receives five vendor emails afterwards, they do not return, and they tell colleagues. The 70% reply rate is the asset at risk, and it is the asset every other line depends on.

### Your chaos point is right, and it gets a real answer
The concern is correct: service providers who believe there is no follow-up mechanism will work the room hard, which is worse for everyone including the funder.

**The answer is consented introductions, made personally.** After the room, ask each commercial guest who they want to continue with. Ask that person whether they are open to it. If yes, make the introduction.

| Included introductions | |
|---|---|
| Service provider seat | 2 |
| Session funder | 3 |
| Co-host | 5 |
| Additional | $250 each, capped at 5 more |

This delivers everything the list was meant to deliver and more:
- It calms the room, because a mechanism visibly exists
- It is legally clean, since consent is explicit and per person
- **A warm introduction from the convener outperforms a cold email to a supplied contact**, so it is worth more to the buyer than the list was
- Every relationship stays routed through us, which is the asset

### The one version of a list that is allowed
**An opt-in list shared among attendees**, where every person actively chooses to appear and everyone who appears also receives it. That is a community feature, not a data sale, and consent is real. It is never given to a funder who was not on it, and it is never a paid deliverable.

### On fairness to free guests
The framing that free admission needs balancing is backwards. **The guests are not receiving something that needs paying for. Their presence is the thing the funder is buying.** The clinician's contribution is showing up. That trade is already balanced.

## Part 2. Venue pricing

**Decision: providing the venue always lowers the price. It never raises it.**

### The contradiction this fixes
`docs/12-convening-as-a-service.md` priced a co-hosted room at $25,000 to $45,000. `docs/14-dinner-programme.md` priced a funded dinner at $3,500 to $7,500. Putting both in one table made them look like rungs of one ladder, which produced an absurd conclusion: **give up your venue and pay more.** That was my error, not a real feature of the model.

The two are different **scopes**, not different **venues**.

### Separate the two dimensions
- **Scope** drives price up. More dinners, an insight brief, media capture, more introductions
- **Venue** drives price down, by the actual cost saved

The financial model already prices this: **$105 a seat in a restaurant, $55 partner-hosted.** On 24 seats, a donated venue saves **$1,200**. That is the credit.

### The corrected structure

| Product | What it includes | Our venue | Their venue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Funded dinner** | 1 dinner, seat, category locked, composition report, 3 introductions | $7,500 | **$6,300** |
| **Dinner plus brief** | Above, plus the five-city insight brief | $22,000 | **$20,800** |
| **Quarterly programme** | 4 dinners, brief, media, 5 introductions, first refusal | $45,000 | **$40,200** |

**Now the logic is consistent.** Hosting is always cheaper than not hosting, for identical scope. A co-host pays more than a session funder only because they bought more, never because they supplied a room.

### Why a co-host takes the deal anyway
Home advantage costs us nothing to give and is worth a great deal to them: their space, their people present, their name on the invitation, their colleagues able to drop in. We buy the venue at cost and give the home-field benefit free, because it is free.

### The rule in one line
**Venue is a credit against price. Scope is the price.**

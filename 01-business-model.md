# Business Model

## Core thesis

The asset is not events. It is a trusted network connecting four groups: **clinicians and researchers**, **founders and operators**, **capital and corporate**, and **policy and government**. Every dollar monetises access to that network's composition, never the dinners themselves.

## The one non-negotiable design rule

**Only monetise the sides that receive commercial value. Never charge the scarce side.**

Clinicians, researchers, academics, students and policy leaders are the reason founders join and the reason corporates pay attention. The moment they are asked to pay, even a token amount, the signal that makes the model work erodes.

Practical implication: free, invitation and nomination based. Free must feel like a curated invitation, never a discount.

## The flywheel

```
vetted paid membership
  → committed, provable room composition
    → clinicians and policy attend because the room is worth an evening
      → composition becomes sellable to corporates
        → funds a better programme
          → more of the right people apply
            → back to the top
```

Each stream makes the others worth more.

## Revenue architecture

| Top tier membership | Scale-ups, firms | $2,950/yr | All-access, highest contribution per member | 1 |
| Guest access | Nobody, ever | Free | The magnet. The signal every paying side buys | 1 |

| Category-exclusive partner | One per vertical | $10–25K/yr | Exclusivity is the premium lever | 2 |

| Summit sponsor | Anchor and rotating | $10–50K | Highest-leverage annual event | 2 |
| Trust-safe services | Corporates | TBD | Only behind conflict-of-interest walls | 3 |

**Bands are anchors to validate, not fixed prices.**

## What makes sponsorship premium

Not headcount. Composition. A sponsor deck should never say "800 members". It says something like:

> "Practising clinicians across dentistry, pharmacy and allied health, funded medtech founders, and health system decision-makers, vetted and in one room."

That claim is only credible because the paid subscription filters for genuine commitment. This is the direct payoff of charging founders even a small amount: it converts a mailing list into an audited asset.

## Sequencing

**Phase 1, months 0–12 — prove the room.** Free guest track and paid founder tier live from the start. Subscription priced as a no-brainer commitment, closer to a gym membership than a SaaS tool. Primary goal is the relationship graph and provable composition, not revenue.

**Phase 2, months 12–24 — formalise.** Once there is a waitlist and repeat attendance data, add team tiers and category-exclusive slots. Clinicians remain free. This never changes.

**Phase 3, month 24+ — layer carefully.** Selective trust-safe lines such as corporate scouting or curated validation, behind strict conflict-of-interest walls. Clinician trust is the product and is never monetised directly, in any phase.

## Guardrails

- **Never charge clinicians, researchers or policy creators.** Cross this line and sponsorship value collapses with it.
- **Price membership as a turnstile, not a product.** The value is the room. The fee creates commitment and should not be judged on ROI alone.
- **Protect category exclusivity.** Selling one vertical twice destroys the premium logic.
- **Do not let phase 3 compromise phase 1 trust.** Any paid advisory or scouting must be visibly walled off from clinical relationships and event curation.

## Why this beats either model alone

**Pure sponsorship** monetises attention, but a free-RSVP audience is cheap unverified inventory. **Pure membership** has a hard cold start and a revenue cap set by headcount. **The hybrid** solves both: the subscription buys the proof that makes sponsorship premium, and sponsorship funds a programme good enough to make the subscription worth paying for.

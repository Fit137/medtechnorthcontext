## Handoff memo, internal and investor use

**Currency:** CAD, exclusive of GST, HST and QST throughout. Tax is collected and remitted, it is not revenue.

---

## 1. What this is and is not

This establishes unit economics, the capacity constraint, three-year shape and the sensitivities that decide the outcome. A complete financial model would add monthly cohort tracking, cash timing, working capital, deferred revenue treatment for annual memberships and a balance sheet. None of that changes the conclusions below, but all of it is needed before a raise.

Every price, unit cost and redemption assumption is carried forward unchanged from the approved membership pricing model. Nothing has been re-rounded or re-derived. Tab 2 Drivers is the only editable surface; the other ten tabs are formula-driven off it.

The honest framing for any external reader: **this model projects a business that has not yet held its first dinner.** Every number is a hypothesis with arithmetic attached. Presented that way it is credible. Presented as a forecast it is not.

## 2. The three findings

### Finding 1: Each chapter has a hard membership ceiling of about 35 paying members

Forty seats per dinner. Half reserved for hosted guests, because the composition rule is one to one builders to validators. That leaves twenty paying seats per evening. At quarterly cadence a city offers eighty paying seat-slots a year, and a typical member attends 2.3 dinners. The ceiling is 80 divided by 2.3, or **34.8 paying members per chapter.**

Five chapters at quarterly cadence cap membership revenue at roughly **$136,000 a year.** That is not a pricing failure, it is the arithmetic of a small room with half its seats given away.

Three consequences follow, and they should shape the operating plan more than any other number in this model:

1. Growth comes from more cities and more evenings per city, never from selling harder into a full chapter.
2. Membership can never be the main revenue line, which is precisely why the sponsorship architecture exists.
3. A waitlist is a feature. It is the scarcity proof that prices the sponsorship.

Cadence is the lever inside a city. Moving to six dinners a year lifts the ceiling to 52 per chapter and five-chapter membership revenue to $205,000. Eight dinners takes it to 70 per chapter and $273,000. Both require a chapter host before they are operationally survivable.

### Finding 2: Membership is under 30 percent of revenue at every stage, sponsorship is about half

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Membership share of revenue | 29.1% | 27.7% | 24.9% |
| Sponsorship share of revenue | 50.1% | 45.8% | 50.1% |

This is the investor story in two lines. Membership is not the product being sold. It is the mechanism that makes the room provable enough to sell sponsorship against. The pricing work was never a revenue exercise, it was a qualification exercise, and the model confirms it.

### Finding 3: The dinner programme is exactly self-funding at base assumptions, and swings $160,000 either side of it

At year-two base assumptions the dinner programme nets to zero: twenty dinners at forty seats and an $80 blended seat cost is $64,000 of delivery, and twenty dinners at an 80 percent sponsor attach with $4,000 cheques is $64,000 of revenue.

The two-way sensitivity in tab 9 shows how violently that moves:

| Partner-hosted share | No sponsor | $2,000 | $4,000 | $6,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% (all restaurant) | $84,000 cost | $44,000 cost | $4,000 cost | $36,000 surplus |
| 50% (base) | $64,000 cost | $24,000 cost | **$16,000 surplus** | $56,000 surplus |
| 100% (all partner-hosted) | $44,000 cost | $4,000 surplus | $36,000 surplus | $76,000 surplus |

A $160,000 annual swing on the dinner programme alone. **No pricing decision in this business is worth as much as venue partnerships and sponsor attach.** That is where founder time should go.

## 3. Unit economics

Contribution per member per year, at typical redemption:

| Tier | Fee | Contribution, unsponsored dinners | Contribution, sponsored dinners | Margin, sponsored |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Guest | $0 | n/a | n/a | This is the magnet, and it costs about $209 a year |
| Resident | $290 | $36 | $220 | 76% |
| Attending | $790 | $348 | $580 | 73% |
| Chief | $2,950 | $1,625 | $2,105 | 71% |

Resident is the line to watch. At $36 of contribution with unsponsored dinners it is effectively a break-even tier, which is the tier-level expression of Finding 3. Chief is the opposite: twelve Chief members contribute more than eighty Residents.

Guest is not a loss line. It is customer acquisition cost for the sponsorship business, and it should be reported that way.

### Lifetime value

| Tier | Annual contribution | Retention (assumed) | Lifetime | LTV | CAC | LTV:CAC | Payback |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Resident | $220 | 65% | 2.9 yrs | $629 | $80 | 7.9x | 4.4 months |
| Attending | $580 | 75% | 4.0 yrs | $2,320 | $250 | 9.3x | 5.2 months |
| Chief | $2,105 | 85% | 6.7 yrs | $14,033 | $900 | 15.6x | 5.1 months |

**Do not quote these as fact.** Retention has no history behind it and is the softest input in the model. Ratios above 7 on every tier suggest one of two things: the assumptions are optimistic, or the pricing is conservative. Given the capacity ceiling, conservative pricing is the more likely explanation, and there is room to test Attending upward at the first renewal cycle.

## 4. Three-year shape

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chapters live | 3 | 5 | 5 |
| Dinners / summits | 12 / 1 | 20 / 2 | 24 / 2 |
| Guest members | 90 | 200 | 280 |
| Paying members | 83 | 150 | 180 |
| Capacity utilisation | 80% | 86% | 86% |
| **Total revenue** | **$207,150** | **$423,380** | **$561,460** |
| Total costs | $137,520 | $300,950 | $413,675 |
| **Net contribution** | **$69,630** | **$122,430** | **$147,785** |
| Net margin | 33.6% | 28.9% | 26.3% |
| Revenue per paying member | $2,496 | $2,823 | $3,119 |

Two things to explain before anyone asks.

**Margin declines and that is intended.** Founder compensation is nil in year one by choice and enters at $60,000 in year two and $130,000 in year three. Contribution before compensation rises throughout. Most models of this type hide the founder cost; this one does not.

**Revenue per paying member rises without any price increase**, from $2,496 to $3,119. Sponsorship per member is the reason. Each additional member makes the room more sellable, and the sponsorship line captures that rather than the membership line.

## 5. Scenarios (year two)

| | Bear | Base | Bull |
|---|---|---|---|
| Partner-hosted share | 25% | 50% | 75% |
| Sponsor attach rate | 50% | 80% | 100% |
| Paying members | 100 | 150 | 180 |
| Category partners | 1 | 3 | 5 |
| Summit tickets and booths | $55,000 | $90,000 | $120,000 |
| Total revenue | $287,000 | $423,000 | $533,000 |
| **Net contribution** | **$31,800** | **$147,200** | **$236,840** |
| Net margin | 11.1% | 34.8% | 44.4% |

**The bear case is the one to defend, and it clears zero.** It assumes half the dinners go unsponsored, only one category partner closes, two chapters underfill and summit revenue lands 39 percent below plan. It still returns $31,800.

Note what is deliberately not flexed: price. All three scenarios hold ARPU constant, because the risk in this business is filling rooms and closing sponsors, not whether $790 is the right number.

At base sponsorship, sponsorship and summit contribution ($208,000) exceeds the entire fixed and compensation base ($122,000) by $86,000. Break-even paying members is therefore zero. Every membership dollar is contribution.

## 6. The four risks that actually matter

Full register in tab 11. These four are High severity and share one root: nothing here has happened yet.

**Retention is unproven.** If Resident retention lands at 40 percent instead of 65, LTV falls by a third and the payback story weakens. Mitigation: run two renewal cycles and report actuals before quoting LTV externally.

**Summit revenue is the weakest line in the model.** Years two and three carry $90,000 and $110,000 on a single blended assumption. Mitigation: rebuild it seat by seat, pass count by type and booth count by tier, before committing to a venue.

**Sponsor attach.** The model needs 80 percent of dinners sponsored or partner-hosted by year two. Mitigation: sell the first four dinner sponsors before scaling the calendar, and treat an unsponsored dinner as a decision not to hold one.

**Founder concentration.** Curation, seating, introductions and sponsor selling all sit with one person. This is the binding operational constraint well before capital is. Mitigation: chapter hosts by year two, and document the composition method so it is transferable.

## 7. What to build next to turn this into a full model

1. **Monthly cohort engine.** Members join on rolling dates; annual memberships create deferred revenue. Model cohorts by join month with per-cohort retention rather than annual averages.
2. **Cash timing.** Sponsorship arrives in lumps, dinner costs are paid on the night, memberships are collected up front. Build a thirteen-week cash view.
3. **Seat-by-seat summit build.** Replace the single blended line with pass types, booth tiers and sponsor cheques.
4. **Chapter unit model.** Make one chapter a repeatable unit with its own P&L, so expansion is modelled as adding units rather than editing totals.
5. **Actuals capture.** After dinner one, replace the assumed blended seat cost, guest-to-payer ratio and attendance rate with measured figures. Three dinners of real data will move this model more than any amount of further modelling.

## 8. Assumption register, ranked by how much damage a wrong answer does

| Assumption | Current value | If wrong by | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sponsor attach rate | 80% by year 2 | Down to 50% | Dinner programme swings to a $24,000 annual cost |
| Partner-hosted share | 50% | Down to 25% | Blended seat cost rises from $80 to $92.50 |
| Summit ticket and booth revenue | $90,000 in year 2 | Down 40% | $36,000 straight off net contribution |
| Resident retention | 65% | Down to 40% | LTV falls from $629 to $367 |
| Average dinners attended | 2.3 of 4 | Up to 3.2 | Chapter ceiling falls from 35 to 25 members |
| Blended CAC | $220 | Up to $400 | $17,000 additional cost in year 2 |

The last one is worth dwelling on. **Higher attendance reduces the chapter ceiling**, because engaged members consume more of the fixed seat supply. Success on engagement is a constraint on membership growth, not a relief from it. Model it as such.

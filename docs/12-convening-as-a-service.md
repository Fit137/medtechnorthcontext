## Product A. Co-hosted convening, at the client's headquarters

**What it is.** MedTech North designs, composes, produces and facilitates a specialty room inside the client's own offices. Theme co-created, guest list built and owned by MedTech North, full production including format, facilitation, panels, media capture and vendors. The client is a co-host, not a sponsor.

**Why co-host is the right word and sponsor is the wrong one.** A sponsor buys attention and is compared against every other place attention can be bought, which means a trade show booth sets the price. A co-host buys a produced outcome and is compared against what it costs them to produce the same thing internally, which is a far higher number and one they already know. The word also survives compliance more comfortably, because a genuine co-production with editorial independence is a recognisable structure, and it changes the budget it comes from: field marketing, events, community or GTM, rather than a sponsorship line.

### Why this is the strongest line available

1. **It is already proven, by Miro, with a public endorsement.** Nothing else in the business has that.
2. **It removes the venue problem entirely.** `ADR-013` already identifies the client's own office as the answer to it. `docs/08-legal-and-compliance.md` treats the condo as the highest unmitigated exposure. Client HQ resolves the commercial-use question, the insurance question and the legitimacy question in one move.
3. **It flips the largest lever in the financial model.** `docs/06-financial-model.md` shows partner-hosted share swinging the dinner programme by roughly $160,000 a year, and blended seat cost falling from $105 to $55. A co-hosted room is 100% partner-hosted by definition. This is the bull case, structurally rather than optimistically.
4. **It repeats with the same client.** A sponsorship is one night. A convening programme is quarterly, which addresses the "retention is entirely unproven" risk head-on.
5. **It has no price anchor in the room's own category.** The comparable is what agency-run field marketing costs them, not what a booth costs.

### The screen that protects the room: topical orthogonality

This is the rule that decides whether a co-host is safe, and it is not obvious.

**The co-host's product must be orthogonal to the room's subject.** Miro worked partly because a collaborative whiteboard convening a patient journey session has no stake in the clinical conclusions. A dental practice-management company co-hosting a room about dental practice management does have a stake, and clinicians will read the room as a sales meeting inside one evening.

| Co-host type | Room subject | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Collaboration, cloud, productivity, design tooling | Any clinical topic | **Safe.** No stake in the conclusions |
| Bank, insurer, accounting firm | Any clinical topic | **Safe on neutrality**, weak on thesis fit. Cap at one |
| Professional services, law | Clinical topic | Safe on neutrality, watch the service-provider cap |
| Dental or pharmacy software vendor | Their own category | **Not safe as co-host.** Sell them the brief or a session instead, with the interest disclosed and bounded |
| Vaccine or drug manufacturer | The condition their product treats | **Never** |

Category-adjacent companies are not lost revenue. They are simply buyers of a different product: the brief, a session, or a clinical advisory roundtable where the commercial interest is declared openly and the format is built to handle it.

### Pricing

| Configuration | Band |
|---|---|
| Single co-hosted specialty room, full production, composition report | $25,000 to $45,000 |
| Four-room annual programme, one specialty per quarter | $90,000 to $150,000 |
| Add the category brief to either | $12,000 to $25,000 |

> **These are scope prices, not venue premiums.** A co-hosted room costs more than a funded dinner because it is a larger production, not because the client supplied a room. Supplying the venue is always a **credit** against the price, worth the $1,200 a donated venue saves on a 24-seat room. Same scope in their building is always cheaper than the same scope in ours. See `ADR-021`.

These sit above the bands in `docs/01-business-model.md`, deliberately. That document already notes the original $20,000 assumption is probably low. This is a produced service with a proven delivery and a marquee reference, not an event sponsorship, and it should not be priced as one.

**The discovery question that sets the price:** "What does it cost you, all in, to produce one executive event today, and what did the last one actually produce?" Their number sets the anchor and it is nearly always higher than expected once staff time, agency fees, venue and travel are counted. Never quote a comparison figure, because no such figure exists in `data/verified-stats.md`.

**Terms:** 50% on signature, 50% on delivery. Pass-through billing for anything outside the fixed scope, agreed in writing before it is incurred.

### The clause that stops this becoming work-for-hire

This is the difference between a service business and an asset that compounds, and it lives entirely in the contract.

MedTech North retains: the guest list, the relationships, the graph data, the intelligence produced, and the right to invite every attendee to its own rooms afterwards. The client receives: the room, the co-host position, a welcome rather than a keynote, the composition report, the media assets and the written summary.

If a client contracts to own the attendee list, the engagement is work-for-hire. The margin may be identical and the network gains nothing. Price that version higher, or decline it.

### What the co-host never receives

Attendee contact data. Guest list visibility before the room. Editorial control over content or the brief. Pitching from the floor. Stage time beyond a welcome. Any claim that clinicians endorse them. Any promised commercial outcome.

## Product B. Canadian market validation, for companies with no Canadian presence

**What it is.** A foreign company that has decided to enter Canada, or is actively evaluating it, buys a compressed beachhead: a composed room of the specialty they need to sell to, structured intelligence on how that specialty actually buys, a map of who matters, and a set of consented introductions. Delivered in six to ten weeks.

**Why this is not a sponsorship and must never be sold as one.** It comes from a market entry, international expansion or new market development budget. Those budgets are strategic, larger, and owned by a VP International, Head of Expansion, CRO or founder.

### Where the earlier "Canadian signature" gate was wrong

`docs/11-corporate-outreach.md` sets three gates and the first says the cheque must be signable by someone holding a Canadian profit and loss statement. **That gate was over-generalised and it is corrected here.**

The reasoning was sound for a $5,000 marketing sponsorship competing for a slot in a Canadian marketing calendar, where a global brand team adds a layer of review with no corresponding budget. It does not hold for a market entry purchase. There, the global team is the natural and only owner, and the absence of a Canadian division **removes** a layer of approval rather than adding one.

**Corrected gate 1: the cheque must be signable by someone who owns the outcome being sold.** For a session, that is a Canadian marketing lead. For market validation, that is an international expansion owner, and no Canadian entity is required or even helpful.

### Why the value is genuinely high, and where the argument overstates itself

**The value case holds.** A foreign entrant cannot convene twenty practising Canadian clinicians in a short window. They have no list, no relationships, no local credibility and no venue. Their alternatives are to fly a team in, hire a consultancy, hire a country manager before knowing whether the market works, or buy a list and cold-call into it. Every one of those costs more than $25,000 and most produce worse information. Pure software entrants face no Health Canada licensing path, so the only real barrier to Canadian revenue is distribution and proof, which is precisely what a composed room supplies.

**There is also no price anchor.** This is the most valuable property of the segment and it is easy to miss. A Canadian dental software company compares the price against a trade show. A Boston company evaluating Canada has nothing to compare it to except its own expansion budget. Pricing power here is the best available anywhere in the business.

**Three places the argument overstates itself, and each has a fix.**

**1. Speed to cash is materially worse, and speed was the stated objective.** A $25,000 expansion decision inside a foreign company runs 60 to 120 days, touches two to four stakeholders, and frequently waits on a planning cycle. `docs/05-monetization.md` already scores the market entry programme at 5 out of 10 on speed for exactly this reason. This is a parallel track, not a substitute for the fast one. Run both, and never let the large slow deal starve the small fast ones of founder attention.

**2. There is no trigger, and no trigger means indefinite slippage.** Canadian targets have a dated 2026 event forcing a decision. "Should we do Canada" is an evergreen question that can always be deferred one quarter. This is the single biggest practical risk in the product. Two fixes, both legitimate. First, use the real dated trigger: the Buy Canadian policy took effect 16 December 2025 with health and pharmaceuticals named a strategic sector, and the Canadian preference threshold dropped from $25M to $5M on 15 June 2026. For a foreign software company, that is a dated, verifiable reason why a Canadian presence matters more this year than last. Second, run fixed cohort dates. A published calendar with a closing date is a manufactured deadline, and manufactured deadlines are how consulting sells.

**3. An event line does not exist in a market entry budget, which is the opposite of the dental advantage.** The dental case is strong precisely because the study club budget line already exists. Foreign entrants have no "convening" line. **The fix is the framing: sell market validation and intelligence, which is an established purchase category, and let the room be the delivery mechanism rather than the product.** Same work, same price, a budget line that already exists.

### The selection screen, which matters more here than anywhere else

Not all foreign companies. The difference between a good and bad fit is roughly a 10x difference in win rate.

| Signal | Why |
|---|---|
| **Already has inbound Canadian customers but no team here** | The decision is made. You are selling execution, not persuasion. Highest win rate on the page |
| **Has publicly announced Canadian or North American expansion** | Same. A stated commitment has an internal owner |
| **Just raised a round with international expansion in the thesis** | Budget exists, clock is running, board is watching |
| **Already operates in a third market beyond its home one** | Expansion is a repeatable motion they know how to buy |
| Pure software, no Health Canada licensing path | Nothing blocks revenue except distribution |
| ACV high enough that 20 to 50 Canadian customers justifies entry | The arithmetic works for them |
| Series B or later | Below that, expansion budget is usually theoretical |

**Disqualifier:** a company still asking whether Canada is worth doing. Educating a prospect into a market entry decision is a twelve-month unpaid sales cycle.

### Pricing

| Configuration | Band |
|---|---|
| Single-specialty validation: one composed room, intelligence, KOL map, consented introductions | $20,000 to $35,000 |
| Multi-room entry programme, three rooms plus intelligence | $60,000 to $120,000 |

Enter at the lower figure deliberately. It sits below the threshold that triggers board or committee approval at most Series B and later companies, which is the same logic as gate 3 in `docs/11-corporate-outreach.md`, applied at a higher absolute number because the budget is different. Expand to the programme after one works.

**Cross-border friction to plan for, not discover:** a W-8BEN-E or equivalent will be requested by US payers, payment terms are frequently net 45 or net 60, wire and FX costs are real, and some buyers require vendor registration before a purchase order can be raised. Ask on the first call who raises the purchase order and what the payment terms are. Separately, services supplied to a non-resident who is not registered in Canada may be zero-rated for GST/HST purposes. **That is a question for an accountant, not an assumption**, and getting it wrong in either direction is expensive.

### Sourcing, which should almost never be cold

The fastest qualified foreign prospects come through organisations whose own performance is measured on foreign direct investment and who cannot convene clinicians themselves. `data/competitors.md` already identifies this.

- **Invest Ontario, Toronto Global, and the regional development agencies.** Their KPIs are landed foreign companies. They hold lists of firms actively evaluating Canada.
- **Foreign trade offices and consulates in Toronto**, and the inbound trade missions they run. These bodies organise delegations to do badly what this product does well.
- **Canadian law and accounting firms with inbound expansion practices.** They incorporate the Canadian entity, so they know precisely who is entering and when, usually months ahead of anyone else.
- **Municipal economic development offices**, including RIC Centre locally.

## On convening 50

The claim that fifty dentists can be convened is credible on the outreach evidence. Two cautions, one operational and one strategic.

**Operationally**, a ~70% positive reply rate on a warm, hand-selected, small-volume list does not extrapolate linearly to a cold specialty list at four times the volume, and a positive reply is not a seat. Room one seated 16, and two confirmed guests withdrew at the confirmation stage. The funnel from interested to seated is where this format will be tested, and it has not been tested at volume in a single specialty. **Sell twenty to twenty-five verified practising dentists with a composition make-good, deliver thirty, then sell fifty.** Over-delivering on the first paid room is worth more than the marginal revenue from promising fifty.

**Strategically**, fifty may be the wrong target even if it is achievable. `data/competitors.md` locates the entire competitive advantage in being small, balanced, neutral and provable, against players whose scale compromises curation. At fifty, proximity degrades and the room starts to resemble the conference format that larger organisations already run better. The defensible move is to price smaller rooms higher, not larger rooms higher. Capacity is a lever to hold in reserve, and it is worth more as demonstrated scarcity than as delivered volume.

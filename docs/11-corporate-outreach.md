## The reframe that decides everything

The instinct is to ask who sponsors health innovation events. The answer to that question is large pharmaceutical and device companies, and they are the slowest, most review-gated, least winnable buyers available to a property with one room behind it.

The right question is different. **Whose customer is already sitting in the room, and who currently pays more to reach that customer through a worse channel?**

The room is dentists, clinical pharmacists, physiotherapists, researchers and students. Not physicians. That single fact rewrites the buyer list, and it improves it. A dentist owns the practice and signs the purchase order. A pharmacy owner does the same. A clinic owner does the same. A hospital physician does none of this. The room as composed carries a higher density of actual economic buyers than a room of hospital physicians would, and it took no additional work to assemble.

So the highest-probability buyer is a company whose sales motion runs direct to a practice owner, whose Canadian marketing budget already funds study clubs and evening education, and whose market changed on 1 July 2026.

## Three gates decide the deal before the pitch starts

Win probability and speed to cash are mostly determined by properties of the buyer, not by how good the pitch is.

**Gate 1: the cheque must be signable by someone who owns the outcome being sold.** For a session or a marketing slot, that means someone holding a Canadian profit and loss statement, because a global brand team cannot approve $5,000 any faster than $500,000. If the answer to "who signs this" sits in New Jersey or Basel and what you are selling is a marketing slot, it is a month-six deal no matter how warm the conversation feels.

> **Corrected 2 August 2026.** This gate originally read "Canadian signature" as a flat rule. That was over-generalised. It holds for a marketing purchase competing for a slot in a Canadian marketing calendar. It does **not** hold for a market entry purchase, where the global team is the natural owner and having no Canadian division removes an approval layer rather than adding one. See `docs/12-convening-as-a-service.md` and `ADR-016`.

**Gate 2: no promotional regulatory review.** Anything requiring PAAB pre-clearance or full medical, legal and regulatory review adds eight to twelve weeks and demands a vendor readiness pack that does not yet exist. Devices, software, services, distribution, finance and insurance carry none of this. Prescription drugs and vaccines carry all of it.

**Gate 3: the cheque fits below the committee line.** In Canadian subsidiary marketing, $3,000 to $10,000 is usually a manager's discretionary decision. Past roughly $25,000 it triggers a committee, a procurement file and a fiscal-year budget cycle. The first deal must land below that line. Category-exclusive conversations belong in month four, after a reference exists.

A company failing all three gates is not a bad company. It is a later company.

## Fix the cash blockers before any outreach begins

A verbal yes is not cash. Five things currently sit between the two, and four of them are already on the `HANDOFF.md` not-done list.

| Blocker | What it stops | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| No registered business name | No compliant invoice carrying a GST/HST number. Corporate accounts payable bounces it | $60 |
| No commercial general liability certificate | Any external venue, and most vendor onboarding | $500 to $1,500/yr estimate |
| No incorporation | Some procurement processes cannot set up a sole proprietorship as a vendor | $300 to $400 DIY |
| No one-page agreement | Their legal team drafts from scratch, which costs weeks | Time only |
| No composition report template | Nothing to deliver, so nothing to invoice against | Time only |

Total cash cost is roughly $400 plus an insurance premium. This is the highest-return week available, because it converts calendar into cash for every deal that follows.

**Payment terms that speed collection:** 100% on signature below $10,000, 50/50 above. Invoice dated on signature, not on delivery. Net 30. Ask on the first call who raises the purchase order and how long their accounts payable cycle runs, because that answer, not the close date, determines when money lands.

## Correct the premise before building on it

The question assumes three proofs. Two of them exist. One does not.

**Proof of concept: yes.** One room, 16 people, 29 July 2026, documented in `data/events/2026-07-29-patient-journey-jam.md`.

**Testimonials: not yet.** The event record says attendees gave positive feedback and were keen to return. `data/verified-stats.md` says no testimonials or endorsements have been consented. Those are compatible statements, and the gap between them is a consent form. Go and collect written, attributable consent from six to eight attendees this week. It is free, it takes an afternoon, and every conversation below is weaker without it.

**Proof of convening per specialty: not yet.** The first room drew ten or more clinicians spread across dentistry, pharmacy and physiotherapy. There is no evidence anywhere in the repo that twelve dentists can be seated in one room. That is the specific claim the entire strategy rests on, and it is currently a hypothesis.

This is not a reason to slow down. It is a reason to sequence correctly. **Run room two as a named dental room, unsponsored, and instrument the outreach funnel obsessively.** Cost is one cycle and roughly $400 of catering. What comes out the other side is the table that `docs/05-monetization.md` correctly identifies as the entire pitch:

> "We contacted 40 dentists, 30 responded positively, 22 confirmed, 19 attended."

A corporate marketing team whose own invitations convert in single digits cannot argue with that table, and it is worth more than any deck. Sell rooms three and four against it. Sell the brief immediately, because the brief carries no composition dependency at all.

## Pick one specialty. Dental.

A mixed room is an audience. A named specialty room is a product with a category, and category is what makes exclusivity worth paying for later.

Dental goes first for six reasons.

1. **The trigger is the largest expansion of federal health coverage in Canadian history.** The CDCP covers more than 6.5 million Canadians, roughly $13 billion over five years, with the 2026-27 benefit year opening 1 July 2026. The benefit grids were revised on 1 April 2026 and service-level rules changed with them. Every dental vendor's roadmap moved this year.
2. **Dentists own their practices.** The room contains buyers, not people who influence a procurement committee.
3. **Dental industry marketing is study-club native.** The budget line, the mechanic and the internal justification already exist. The ask is a reallocation, not a new budget, which is a materially easier internal conversation for the person you are asking.
4. **Almost no PAAB exposure.** Dental buyers sell devices, materials, software and services.
5. **Dense Ontario supplier base** inside a ninety-minute drive of Mississauga.
6. **The first room already contained dentists and a dental hygiene student.** Composition is credible rather than aspirational.

Pharmacy runs second on a slower clock, because its largest buyers are its slowest. The 1 July 2026 scope expansion to 28 minor ailments, reaching 33 in early 2027, plus six newly publicly funded vaccines, is a genuine commercial earthquake. It is also the trigger that most directly benefits vaccine manufacturers, who sit behind both regulatory gates. Sell pharmacy the brief now. Sell pharmacy a room in month four.

## The offer that maximises both variables at once

Do not sell a single event as a standalone. The ratio is poor: a $5,000 cheque carrying a six-week sales cycle. Sell two lines in one conversation, where the room is the cheap and easy yes attached to a research purchase.

**Line 1: the specialty session.** $3,500 to $7,500. Theme co-created, a welcome rather than a keynote, one seat in the room, a written composition report afterwards. Funds the room. Drawn from marketing budget.

**Line 2: the category brief.** $12,000 to $25,000 commissioned with 90-day exclusivity, or $3,500 syndicated to six to eight buyers. Built from the interviews already scheduled plus what the room produces. Aggregated and anonymised, nobody named. Drawn from market research or market development budget.

The pair wins because the two lines draw on different budgets, clear different review paths, and fail independently. The brief contains no promotional content whatsoever, which in many organisations reduces it to a purchase order rather than a review. It also delivers in six weeks at near-zero cost, and it is the artifact that makes the third and fourth conversation easy.

**Fastest realistic path to first cash: syndicate the dental brief.** Six buyers at $3,500 is $21,000, invoiced on order, delivered in six weeks, with no venue, no insurance dependency and no promotional review. Attach one session at $5,000 to whichever of the six is warmest. Compare that against chasing a single $25,000 category-exclusive commitment at roughly one-in-four odds across a 90 to 120 day cycle. Many small fast cheques beat one large slow one, and they also produce five reference conversations instead of one.

### The clause that closes an unproven property

State the composition target in writing. For example: a minimum of twelve practising dentists, two dental hygienists, and no more than two service providers. Deliver a composition report afterwards. Then offer a make-good: if the stated composition is not met, the next session is included at no charge.

This costs nothing when you deliver. It removes the buyer's entire downside, and it is the single most effective answer to "you have only run one event." Note the boundary carefully. Composition is a promise about who is in the room, and it is deliverable. A commercial outcome is not, and per `docs/08-legal-and-compliance.md` it must never be promised.

### Price the first one at the bottom of the band, out loud

"This is the first session of its kind and the rate reflects that. In exchange I need two things: a reference conversation afterwards, and permission to describe the arrangement privately to other prospects."

Stated as a trade rather than a discount, a founding rate raises win probability sharply and produces the reference that prices everything after it. Stated as a discount, it sets a ceiling you will spend a year climbing back off.

## Channel order, by conversion rate

**1. Clinician nomination. Free, and it converts better than anything else.** Ask every attendee from room one: "Which company should be in this room?" `docs/10-growth-flywheel.md` already establishes why this question works. It is flattering rather than extractive, it monetises their judgement rather than their contacts, and a clinician naming their practice software vendor gives you the warmest possible opening: their own customer asked for them to be there. Do this before any cold outreach.

**2. The interview pipeline doubles as buyer discovery.** Ten to twelve clinician interviews are already the next action for the brief. Add two questions to each one. "Which three companies are working hardest to reach you right now, and how?" and "What did the last vendor evening you attended get wrong?" The output is simultaneously the target list, the competitive intelligence and the pitch, gathered at zero marginal cost.

**3. Warm employer paths** through attendees already inside relevant organisations.

**4. Researched cold outreach.** A named individual, referencing their specific 2026 trigger by date, offering the brief's table of contents as the opening rather than an ask.

## The pitch mechanic

Never sell headcount. `docs/01-business-model.md` is explicit: a deck should never say "800 members," it should describe composition. With one room held, it should not say a number at all.

The central move is to make the prospect do the arithmetic themselves. Ask: **"What did you spend on your last trade show, and how many conversations with practice owners did it actually produce?"**

This is the best sales call available, for three reasons. It answers the open pricing question in `HANDOFF.md` about what corporate convening is really worth. It surfaces the prospect's real budget and their real frustration with their current channel. And it lets them arrive at the cost-per-verified-conversation comparison without you asserting a single figure, which matters because no trade show cost figure appears in `data/verified-stats.md` and therefore none may be quoted.

Small is the feature, not the apology. For a company whose product needs explaining, fifteen attentive practice owners beats several hundred badge scans, and the composition report makes that defensible inside their own reporting.

The reason they cannot build this themselves is the reason they pay. A vendor convening its own customers holds a sales meeting, and clinicians detect it inside one evening. A neutral convener holds a conversation. That neutrality is the product, which is why `docs/05-monetization.md` draws the line where it does: hosting is fine, curating is not.

## What is never sold, written down so it is not improvised away under pressure

- No attendee list, no contact data, no lead capture, no badge scanning
- No stage time and no pitching from the floor
- No editorial control over the brief, no product mentions inside its content
- No shared category, including with a subsidiary
- No guest list visibility before the room
- No suggestion that clinicians, institutions or regulators endorse anything
- No promised commercial outcome

If a prospect's core requirement is leads, say so kindly and end the conversation. That is the wrong buyer, and taking their money costs more than it pays.

## Ninety-day sequence

| When | Move |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | Register the business name. Get CGL quotes. Decide incorporation. Build the invoice template, the one-page agreement and the composition report template. Collect written testimonial consent from six to eight attendees. Ask room one the nomination question |
| Weeks 1 to 3 | Ten to twelve clinician interviews with the two buyer-discovery questions added. Build the target list from their answers plus `data/corporate-targets.md` |
| Weeks 2 to 4 | Outreach to Tier A. Budget 25 named companies to produce 8 conversations to produce 3 or 4 closes |
| Week 4 | Date the dental room and announce it to clinicians **before** any commercial conversation concludes. The room happens regardless. Never make the room contingent on a cheque |
| Weeks 4 to 6 | Close four to six brief syndicate buyers. Close one session partner |
| Weeks 6 to 8 | Run the dental room. Capture the funnel table. Deliver the brief and the composition report |
| Weeks 8 to 12 | Convert those into references. Open pharmacy. Open the first category-exclusive conversations at $10,000 to $25,000, now priced against a real table rather than a hypothesis |

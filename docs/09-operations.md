# Operations

## Venues

### The condo amenity — Mississauga

Owned access, booked monthly for nine months, zero cost. Capacity 50, but 50 requires a $500 security deposit plus hired security at ~$50/hr (~$250/night).

**Recommended working capacity: 25 to 35.** Fifty people in a residential building with hired security is precisely the configuration that generates a complaint, and 35 in a room built for 50 feels abundant while 50 feels tight. Composition quality also drops as size rises.

**Amenities and their best use:**

| Amenity | Best use |
|---|---|
| Large TV with laptop hookup | Founder presentations, live journey mapping |
| High-end sofas | 2 to 3 person recorded panels. **The killer use case:** zero venue cost, produces sellable inventory without a sponsor entering the room |
| Well-lit boardroom, capacity 8 | One-on-one clinician interviews. Highest-volume content source, bookable any weekday |
| Main lounge | The monthly core room, 25 to 35 |

**Recording rule:** anything a sponsor pays for gets recorded remotely or at an external site. Unsponsored content can be recorded in the building freely. Remote sidesteps the question entirely and removes the once-a-month cap.

⚠️ **The venue cost a confirmed physician speaker.** For an MD, a residential venue is a legitimacy test before it is a logistics detail. Use it for unsponsored rooms; move anything requiring institutional legitimacy elsewhere.

### External venues

**Best option: the sponsor's own office.** Costs nothing, removes the commercial-use problem entirely, gives them home-field advantage, and the guest list still belongs to you.

**Others:** donated boardrooms from law firms, banks, cloud providers, hospital innovation offices, university innovation hubs. **These are exactly the category-partner verticals.** Asking a firm to host converts the venue problem into the sponsor pipeline. Hosting is the per-dinner sponsor tier paid in space instead of cash.

**Restaurant format:** $105/head benchmark. Run at least one to convert the $105 assumption from a guess into a measurement.

### Patient Journey Jam

The format used at the first event and worth repeating. Founders map their product's patient journey live on a pre-built Miro frame rather than a blank canvas. The room annotates in real time and prioritises where friction actually sits. Presenters leave with their annotated map, exported.

**Why it works:** it produces a tangible artifact for the presenter, it gives clinicians something to do rather than watch, and it surfaces cross-disciplinary disagreement, which is the most valuable output.

## Room mechanics

**Composition target:** 1:1 builders to validators. Guest list composed 3 to 4 weeks out against a target.

**Seating is the product.** Placed by hand so each person sits beside the two or three most relevant to what they are working on.

**Follow-up introductions within the week**, personally. This is the retention engine and the most commonly skipped step.

**Chatham House by default.**

---

## Registration and ratio control

**Luma mechanics for composition control:**

- **Approval-required registration.** Registration becomes an application, not a purchase, so nothing is committed until you allocate. This is what makes mid-flight rebalancing possible
- **Hold-back:** open ~60% of seats publicly, hold 40% to correct the mix in the final fortnight
- **Hidden ticket types** unlocked by code for paid builder tickets, so the seat count never advertises an imbalance
- The Supabase `member_type` taxonomy tags each registrant by role, so pending-list composition is readable before approving anyone

**The critical distinction: the ratio governs recruitment, not seating.** Never turn a clinician away because the ratio is off. Pause clinician outreach and push builder outreach instead. Per-room composition and pipeline ratio are two different clocks.

## Proof of concept economics

Six monthly rooms, 25 seats, Mississauga, light catering ~$30/head:

| | Cost | Builder ticket revenue | Net |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rooms 1–2 (design partner phase) | $1,500 + $600 print | $0 | −$2,100 |
| Rooms 3–6 (~12 builders × $70) | $900/room | $840/room | ≈−$60/room |
| Contingency 15% | | | −$400 |
| **Total at risk over 6 months** | | | **≈$2,700** |

**The entire proof of concept costs $2,500–5,000.** The same six rooms as Toronto restaurant dinners at 40 seats would cost $25,200.

**From room 3 the programme roughly self-funds**, because twelve builder tickets at $70 cover catering for all 25 people.

**One variable to nail down:** whether the lounge permits outside catering or imposes a minimum spend. Nothing else in the plan comes close to it in leverage.

## PoC exit criteria

All five before claiming proof:
- 4+ rooms held
- 50%+ repeat attendance
- 3+ institutions represented per room
- 2+ documented outcomes
- 8–12 builders having paid something

## Timeline

| Month | Room | Move |
|---|---|---|

| 1 | 2 | Same. Composition record begins. First recorded panel |
| 2 | 3 | Builder tickets open. Soft sponsor conversations, asking for input not pitching |
| 3 | 4 | **Publish composition report #1.** Formal pitches start. Founding memberships open |
| 4–5 | 5–6 | Convert builders. Underwriter conversations mature |
| 6 | — | First underwriter closes. External Toronto room opens, funded |

**Sell in parallel, never after.** Sponsorship cycles run two to four months. But do not pitch before holding one artifact; the composition report at room 4 is the entry ticket.

⚠️ **Do not let the clinician-only phase run past three rooms.** A clinician room with no builders under a medtech brand eventually has no reason to exist, and guests feel the absence before you do.
